## 1. Summary

What a driver earns per hour depends mostly on how many other idle cars are close by when a request appears, and on the platform's matching rule, more than on anything the driver does alone. New York's market is large and lopsided: about 665k app trips a day in 2025 (Uber about 72%, Lyft about 28%), about 130k yellow-taxi trips a day from about 10,500 active medallions, and 82,225 licensed app vehicles. Our own estimate is about 15k app cars online on average [S/design]. The public app trip records carry no driver or vehicle ID, so competitor supply cannot be replayed. It has to be a calibrated population of bot drivers, fitted to the TLC monthly aggregates (unique drivers, vehicles per day, hours per vehicle) and to request-to-on-scene times by zone and hour. The variables with the most effect on profit per hour are the local ratio of idle cars to requests, the ping rate, utilisation (about 59% for NYC app cars), pickup time and the 'wild goose chase' regime when supply is thin, airport queue length and wait, how much of the offer a driver sees before accepting, how quickly rivals log on during surge, and platform log-on lockouts. Several NYC rules shape supply directly: the pause on new FHV licences since 2018; the 2024 Uber and Lyft lockouts, driven by a pay formula tied to utilisation; yellow cabs' monopoly on street hails; the 4-5 pm taxi shift change; and airport first-in-first-out queues with taxi short-return tickets. Robotaxis cannot carry passengers in NYC (Waymo's test permit lapsed on 31 Mar 2026), so robotaxi presence is a scenario setting. Waymo's US average of about 20 trips per car per day gives a realism check, and Uber's dispatch of Waymo cars in Austin and Atlanta is the template for mixed fleets. At fleet and multi-agent level, the fleet's share of total supply decides whether its cars just take the market as it is or change it, and the fleet's own cars take trips from each other. Scores should be reported under both fixed and responsive background supply. ## 2. Recommended way to generate background supply

1. **Demand first, supply as bots.** Resample requests from TLC trip records (R05). Represent rivals as a population of bot drivers, because public app records have no driver or vehicle ID and cannot be chained into shifts [S, columns read on GitHub].
2. **Fix the totals from TLC monthly aggregates.** Unique drivers, vehicles per day and average hours per vehicle per day give citywide vehicle-hours by month and licence class (yellow, green, app). Split app supply by company using trip shares.
3. **Fit the spatial and hourly shape to pickup times.** Choose the idle-supply field per zone and 15-minute slot so that simulated request-to-on-scene times match the HVFHV distribution for that zone and hour. The wild goose chase model shows pickup time falls steeply as idle density rises, so this mapping is informative [P].
4. **Give bots behaviour, not just positions.** Use an archetype mix (hotspot parker, cruiser, airport queuer, surge chaser, home-anchored commuter, cherry-picker). Add log-on and log-off hazards (hours plus income target), short-run entry during surge, and acceptance rules. Fit shift-length and stopping parameters on the 2010-2013 driver-ID taxi data, then carry them forward [U].
5. **Conserve requests (displacement).** When the agent takes a request, the bot that would have served it stays idle and re-plans. Under 'fixed supply' bots do not respond to the agent; under 'elastic supply' they do. Report both [design].
6. **Validate on held-out months** against utilisation (~59%), the Manhattan empty share (~1/3), idle gaps (8-11 min), airport queue waits (1-3 h) and driver_pay per hour from HVFHV [S].
7. **Keep platform internals as declared constants** (batch window, dispatch radius, offer timeout), because they are not public. Run sensitivity sweeps so model rankings do not hinge on one guess [design].

## 5. Key interactions with other domains

- R05 Riders and demand: the ratio of idle cars to requests (R06) is demand divided by supply. Rider patience and cancellation when pickup times are long (R05) close the wild-goose-chase loop.
- R07 Fares, pay and platform rules: surge (R07) is the signal that pulls rivals in and moves them around (R06). The TLC minimum-pay formula divides pay by company utilisation, which is why the platforms locked drivers out. The JFK flat fare and airport fees set what an airport queue is worth.
- R03 Events and disruptions: rivals crowd venue exits, which wears away event surge. Strikes are supply events. In the with/without event-information test, the bots' knowledge of events must be set to match the agent's, or the comparison is biased.
- R04 Weather: in rain, demand jumps while supply stays roughly flat (Farber). Heavy snow takes drivers off the road. Robotaxi operating limits suspend robotaxi supply in bad weather.
- R02 Time and calendar: shift-start curves, the 4-5 pm taxi shift change, holidays, and Ramadan or Friday-prayer dips all depend on the calendar.
- R01 Geography: zone polygons for the supply field, street-hail boundaries (the Manhattan core where only yellow cabs may hail), airport lots and geofences, drivers' home boroughs, and foot traffic for hail encounters.
- R10 Traffic and routing: road travel times are the matching cost and set pickup time. Cruising empty cars add to congestion, so supply feeds back into traffic.
- R08 Vehicle and energy: vehicle class sets which products a car can take (XL, Comfort Electric, wheelchair-accessible). EV and accessible cars are exempt from the licence pause. Robotaxi charging downtime cuts robotaxi supply.
- R09 Driver as a person and agent decisions: the agent's own choices about multi-apping, accepting offers, the destination filter ('take me home') and airport queuing all act against the rival behaviour modelled here. Rivals' breaks mirror the agent's breaks.
- R11 Fleet of N cars: own-fleet cannibalisation, whether the platform or the fleet picks which car takes a trip, rebalancing against background supply, and the fleet's share of the market.
- R12 Benchmark settings and data: how background supply is generated, whether it responds to the agent, what the agent can see of rivals, common random numbers and seat rotation for multi-agent runs, and the public calibration targets.

## 7. Open questions

1. Are Uber and Lyft still locking drivers out, or running waitlists, in NYC as of Oct 2026, and did the TLC act on the NYTWA petition about lockout-period utilisation data?
2. Exact 2025-26 TLC monthly values (unique drivers, vehicles per day, hours per vehicle) are needed to fix the size of the supply field. The host was blocked here, so download the monthly CSV before Sunday's component meeting.
3. Is inferring idle supply from request-to-on-scene times by zone and hour precise enough? Validate on a month where Chicago or the 2013 driver-ID data allow a cross-check.
4. Platform internals are not public: batch window, dispatch radius, offer timeout in NYC, forward-dispatch look-ahead, and whether acceptance rate changes dispatch priority. Should they be fixed design constants with sensitivity analysis, or a hidden randomised rule that agents must learn?
5. Should background bots get the same event information as the agent in the with/without event-awareness test? Asymmetric information changes how big the measured effect of event information is.
6. Should agent fleets displace background supply or add to it? This decides whether fleet-level scores are realistic for N in the hundreds or thousands.
7. Robotaxi scenarios: what share of supply to assume for a 'future NYC' and which operating limits (no freeways, weather pauses) to impose, given state law currently forbids driverless passenger service.
8. The NYC multi-apping share, green taxi active count and taxi e-hail share were not found. They need TLC data or a driver survey.
9. Holiday, Ramadan and Friday-prayer supply dips are only anecdotal (Paris 2017). Measure them in HVFHV on-scene times before treating them as more than low impact.
10. Rules for NYC TLC drivers picking up at Newark (EWR) and elsewhere in NJ, and their effect on airport strategy, are unverified.
11. Exact TLC driver fatigue caps (hours of passenger service per 24 h and per week) need confirming; the figures here are from memory.
