## 1. Summary

What a driver earns per hour depends mostly on how many other idle cars are close by when a request appears, and on the platform's matching rule, more than on anything the driver does alone. New York's market is large and lopsided: about 665k app trips a day in 2025 [corrected by fact-check: 243,589,684 trips, about 667k/day] (Uber about 72%, Lyft about 28%), about 130k yellow-taxi trips a day from about 10,500 active medallions, and 82,225 licensed app vehicles. Our own estimate is about 15k app cars online on average [S/design]. The public app trip records carry no driver or vehicle ID, so competitor supply cannot be replayed. It has to be a calibrated population of bot drivers, fitted to the TLC monthly aggregates (unique drivers, vehicles per day, hours per vehicle) and to request-to-on-scene times by zone and hour. The variables with the most effect on profit per hour are the local ratio of idle cars to requests, the ping rate, utilisation (about 59% for NYC app cars), pickup time and the 'wild goose chase' regime when supply is thin, airport queue length and wait, how much of the offer a driver sees before accepting, how quickly rivals log on during surge, and platform log-on lockouts. Several NYC rules shape supply directly: the pause on new FHV licences since 2018; the 2024 Uber and Lyft lockouts, driven by a pay formula tied to utilisation [corrected by fact-check: since 1 Aug 2025 the pay floor uses fixed applied utilisation rates, and 35 RCNY 59D-22(c) requires 72 h notice before a day's restriction and at least 16 h of access once a driver logs on]; yellow cabs' monopoly on street hails; the 4-5 pm taxi shift change; and airport first-in-first-out queues with taxi short-return tickets. Robotaxis cannot carry passengers in NYC (Waymo's test permit lapsed on 31 Mar 2026), so robotaxi presence is a scenario setting. Waymo's US average of about 20 trips per car per day gives a realism check, and Uber's dispatch of Waymo cars in Austin and Atlanta is the template for mixed fleets. At fleet and multi-agent level, the fleet's share of total supply decides whether its cars just take the market as it is or change it, and the fleet's own cars take trips from each other. Scores should be reported under both fixed and responsive background supply. ## 2. Recommended way to generate background supply

1. **Demand first, supply as bots.** Resample requests from TLC trip records (R05). Represent rivals as a population of bot drivers, because public app records have no driver or vehicle ID and cannot be chained into shifts [S, columns read on GitHub].
2. **Fix the totals from TLC monthly aggregates.** Unique drivers, vehicles per day and average hours per vehicle per day give citywide vehicle-hours by month and licence class (yellow, green, app). Split app supply by company using trip shares.
3. **Fit the spatial and hourly shape to pickup times.** Choose the idle-supply field per zone and 15-minute slot so that simulated request-to-on-scene times match the HVFHV distribution for that zone and hour [corrected by fact-check: only Uber rows carry on_scene_datetime; Lyft fills it for about 0.3% of trips, so this target is Uber-only]. The wild goose chase model shows pickup time falls steeply as idle density rises, so this mapping is informative [P].
4. **Give bots behaviour, not just positions.** Use an archetype mix (hotspot parker, cruiser, airport queuer, surge chaser, home-anchored commuter, cherry-picker). Add log-on and log-off hazards (hours plus income target), short-run entry during surge, and acceptance rules. Fit shift-length and stopping parameters on the 2010-2013 driver-ID taxi data, then carry them forward [U].
5. **Conserve requests (displacement).** When the agent takes a request, the bot that would have served it stays idle and re-plans. Under 'fixed supply' bots do not respond to the agent; under 'elastic supply' they do. Report both [design].
6. **Validate on held-out months** against utilisation (~59%), the Manhattan empty share (~1/3), idle gaps (8-11 min), airport queue waits (1-3 h) and driver_pay per hour from HVFHV [S].
7. **Keep platform internals as declared constants** (batch window, dispatch radius, offer timeout), because they are not public. Run sensitivity sweeps so model rankings do not hinge on one guess [design].

## 5. Key interactions with other domains

- R05 Riders and demand: the ratio of idle cars to requests (R06) is demand divided by supply. Rider patience and cancellation when pickup times are long (R05) close the wild-goose-chase loop.
- R07 Fares, pay and platform rules: surge (R07) is the signal that pulls rivals in and moves them around (R06). The TLC minimum-pay formula divides pay by company utilisation, which is why the platforms locked drivers out [corrected by fact-check: true until Jul 2025; since 1 Aug 2025 fixed applied rates are used (53.3% per minute, 68.5% per mile) and lockouts are limited by 35 RCNY 59D-22(c)]. The JFK flat fare and airport fees set what an airport queue is worth.
- R03 Events and disruptions: rivals crowd venue exits, which wears away event surge. Strikes are supply events. In the with/without event-information test, the bots' knowledge of events must be set to match the agent's, or the comparison is biased.
- R04 Weather: in rain, demand jumps while supply stays roughly flat (Farber). Heavy snow takes drivers off the road. Robotaxi operating limits suspend robotaxi supply in bad weather.
- R02 Time and calendar: shift-start curves, the 4-5 pm taxi shift change, holidays, and Ramadan or Friday-prayer dips all depend on the calendar.
- R01 Geography: zone polygons for the supply field, street-hail boundaries (the Manhattan core where only yellow cabs may hail), airport lots and geofences, drivers' home boroughs, and foot traffic for hail encounters.
- R10 Traffic and routing: road travel times are the matching cost and set pickup time. Cruising empty cars add to congestion, so supply feeds back into traffic.
- R08 Vehicle and energy: vehicle class sets which products a car can take (XL, Comfort Electric, wheelchair-accessible). EV and accessible cars are exempt from the licence pause. Robotaxi charging downtime cuts robotaxi supply.
- R09 Driver as a person and agent decisions: the agent's own choices about multi-apping, accepting offers, the destination filter ('take me home') and airport queuing all act against the rival behaviour modelled here. Rivals' breaks mirror the agent's breaks.
- R11 Fleet of N cars: own-fleet cannibalisation, whether the platform or the fleet picks which car takes a trip, rebalancing against background supply, and the fleet's share of the market.
- R12 Benchmark settings and data: how background supply is generated, whether it responds to the agent, what the agent can see of rivals, common random numbers and seat rotation for multi-agent runs, and the public calibration targets.

**Key corrections.**
1. **Log-on lockouts (R06-054; summary; section 5).** Since 1 Aug 2025, 35 RCNY 59D-22(c) has set these rules:
   - A platform must give 72 h notice before restricting a driver on a calendar day.
   - A driver who logs on must stay available for at least 16 h. The 16 h restart after 8 h offline.
   - Exceptions cover licensing, rule breaches, misconduct, and excessive cancellation or non-acceptance.
   - The fine is $500 per violation.

   The pay floor now divides by fixed applied rates of 53.3% (per minute) and 68.5% (per mile), not by each company's actual utilisation. Mid-shift lockouts are therefore barred for post-Aug-2025 scenarios, and the model is now vintage-dependent.
2. **Fatigue caps (R06-009; open question 11).** The rule is 10 h of passenger time in any 24 h, and the count resets after 8 h with no passengers. There is also a cap of 60 h per Monday-Sunday week. Time between trips does not count (35 RCNY 80-14(f)).
3. **Pickup-time calibration (R06-001, 038, 069; section 2 step 3).** Lyft (HV0005) fills on_scene_datetime on only about 0.3% of trips, so public request-to-on-scene times are Uber-only. 4. **2025 app volume (R06-001, 003, 035).** The 2025 HVFHV records hold 243,589,684 trips, about 667k a day: Uber 176.0M (72%) and Lyft 67.6M (28%). The '~1,060 other bases' figure is a cumulative base list kept in a GitHub repo, not a count of bases active in 2025.
5. **Yellow cabs on the road (R06-001, 004).** The earlier figure of 5-8k in a daytime hour is too high for about 130k trips a day. Design arithmetic gives about 3-5k; check it against TLC monthly vehicles_per_day and hours.
6. **Airport pickups (R06-045).** 2025 app pickups were 4.92M at LGA and 4.07M at JFK, about 25k a day combined. These are the top two app pickup zones by revenue.
7. **Taxi refusal and information (R06-025).** Cabs must take riders to any destination in NYC, Nassau, Westchester or Newark Airport. They may not ask the destination before the rider is seated, except on Flex Fare trips or when signed off duty going home (35 RCNY 80-20, 80-19). Taxis have a legal 'going home' mode, now noted in R06-028.
8. **Green taxi zones (R06-005, 032).** The Hail Exclusionary Zone is Manhattan south of E 96th St and W 110th St, plus the NYC airports. Green cars also may not start pre-arranged trips in that Manhattan zone (35 RCNY 51-03, 82-13). The dossier's statement was right; its tag moved from [U] to [P*].
9. **WAV, shared and Access-A-Ride fields (R06-007, 030, 031).** The raw column names are wav_request_flag, wav_match_flag, access_a_ride_flag, shared_request_flag and shared_match_flag. In 2025:
   - WAV vehicles served about 9% of app trips (21.4M).
   - Explicit WAV requests that were matched were about 0.3% (699k).
   - Shared requests were 2.8% (all Uber), and 1.6% matched.
   - Accessible taxis must accept Accessible Taxi Dispatcher jobs (35 RCNY 53-08).
10. **Robotaxis (R06-056, 057, 059).** The NYC permit facts match an independent GitHub source. The US fleet was about 3,000 cars in early 2026, and the 3,800 figure is unconfirmed. The trips-per-car range is now 19-24 a day. The Austin fleet counts of about 100 in 2025 and about 200 in 2026 are analyst estimates, not Waymo disclosures.
