# Secret strategy games best separate AI models

*Research report, 1 Oct 2026. Evidence base: the fact-checked notes in `research/research_notes/Novel AI benchmark and game ideas/` (paths below are relative to that folder).*

The strongest idea is **Season Forge**, an A2 benchmark and genuinely complex game in which every model writes a bot for a brand-new, secret, multi-player fog-of-war strategy game each season, rated over thousands of games against a fixed ladder of reference bots. It is the only finalist that survived both adversarial review and a blinded pilot with its premise intact: Claude tiers spread from **0.41 ladder rungs (Haiku) to 5.08 (Opus)**, Opus's bot beat the strongest sealed reference bot in **87%** of games, and the round-2 exploit reviewer ranked it the hardest design to game. For the archetype where humans beat AI, the best option is **Stump Arena**, a renewing crowd-authoring benchmark that measures how cheaply ordinary people can find items they solve easily and a given model fails; it was the value reviewer's top pick but is unpiloted. **Unrun Lab** (forecasting interventions in secret simulators against exact rollout truth) ranks third and **Compaction Chronicle** (memory over two-million-token streams) is a conditional fourth. Three other finalists failed their own pilots, a finding in itself: clean visual rule induction, refactor-versus-sabotage auditing and self-knowledge quizzes no longer separate mid- and top-tier models from each other or from humans. On your hypothesis, lack of novelty explains contamination failures, but benchmarks built on novel items and skills (ARC-AGI-3, FrontierMath Tier 4) still fell within months; what failed benchmarks share is a cheap path to a higher score that bypasses the named ability, plus no owner renewing the task faster than labs optimise against it. All pilot evidence comes from one lab's models in an unfrozen harness with tiny samples, and many external figures rest on search summaries because primary hosts were blocked.

## Four designs survive review, and only one also survived its pilot

The project generated 47 candidates, scored them with five independent red teams (235 verdicts: 53 keep, 142 revise, 40 kill), cut 16, absorbed 6 and selected 7 finalists. Two further reviewers then attacked the revised specs, one as a lab trying to game them (R6) and one as a measurement and value skeptic (R7), and five finalists were built as blinded pilots solved by four Claude tiers (`phase4/revision_finalists.md`, `phase4/pilot/PILOT_SUMMARY.md`).

The pilots overturned the provisional ranking (F2 > F1 > F3 > F7 > F6 > F4 > F5), which predated them: its first, third and seventh picks failed, and its sixth pick produced the only strong positive result. R7 ranked Stump Arena first, but R7 did not see the pilots, and Season Forge is the only shortlisted idea with measured separation and the best exploit-robustness rating. If you specifically want a human-beats-AI headline, Stump Arena is the pick, with the caveat that its A1 gap is a forecast, not a measurement.

## The shortlist: what to build and what must change

Cost figures are the specs' own estimates at $4/$20 per million input/output tokens, thinking included; claims taken from the round-2 reviews R6 and R7, which were not independently fact-checked, are marked **[R6]** or **[R7]**; **[S]** marks a source seen only as a search summary or secondary copy.

### 1. Season Forge: write a bot for a game nobody has seen

| Field | Design |
|---|---|
| Archetype | **A2**, complex game. AI already beats elite humans at timed heuristic contests, so the value is model-to-model separation |
| Task format | Each season a new 2–4-player, simultaneous-move territory and economy game with fog of war, 200–500 turns and hidden but inferable dynamics. Entrants get rules text, an engine binary, a local runner and 5 public maps, then write a bot in a frozen 8-core sandbox with no internet. Standard budget ≤ 8M billed output tokens and 48 core-hours; Lite track ≤ 1M tokens and 4 core-hours |
| Novel instances | A new game per season from a private design grammar; evaluation maps generated per match after submissions lock; engine released after the season into a legacy league |
| Scoring | Bradley–Terry rating with reference-ladder ratings held fixed (MCTS bots at doubling playout budgets, an operator bot built with 10× an entrant's budget, later a frozen champion ensemble). ≥ 2,000 games per bot, seats rotated, map seeds duplicated; headline is the mean over pre-registered attempts with bootstrap CIs. Human percentile, cost and tokens are secondary |
| Expected human vs AI | Frontier bots beat the median contest veteran [speculation, anchored]: models can run thousands of local games and iterate for 48 hours, against humans' 6. The strongest human-written bots may still lead and are the natural top anchors |
| Expected model separation | Wide between tiers (pilot); frontier neighbours separate only with enough attempts, because attempt-to-attempt variance dominates game-level noise |
| Must-fix and risks | Stronger top anchors (search bots, expert human-programmer bots), since Opus already overshot the ladder; 8–10 attempts per model instead of 5 [R7]; a frozen per-move compute cap for bots and anchors, without which play-time search buys rating [R6]; a stated engine-access policy [R7]; never seat same-lab bots together [R6]; randomised anchor seeds and budgets [R6]; engine served out-of-sandbox or with per-match hidden parameters [R6]; Plackett–Luce with seat effects for 3–4-player games [R7]. Cost **$1.5–4k per model per season** (5 Standard attempts), $200–600 Lite, plus $20–50k a year for an in-person human season [estimates]. Upkeep of a new game each season and weak lab adoption of game arenas are the strategic risks |

**Pilot evidence.** The pilot game, "Brackwater", was a 2-player, 200-turn version with a hidden periodic tide that floods rich territory; solvers worked under a Lite-like budget (about 1M output tokens, 4 core-hours) with a play-time cap of 0.25 CPU-seconds per move (`phase4/pilot/F4/public/INSTRUCTIONS.md`, `API.md`). The sealed ladder was steep: each rung beat the one below in 76–100% of 400 games per pair, and scoring all four bots took 163 seconds on 4 cores.

| Solver | vs random | vs L1 | vs L2 | vs L3 (operator bot) | Rung position [95% CI] | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opus | 1.00 | 0.98 | 0.98 | **0.87** | **5.08** [4.62, 5.61], extrapolated above ladder | interrupted snapshot |
| Sonnet | 1.00 | 0.31 | 0.03 | 0.00 | 0.81 [0.74, 0.87] | interrupted snapshot |
| Fable | 1.00 | 0.29 | 0.01 | 0.00 | 0.79 [0.71, 0.85] | interrupted, early version |
| Haiku | 0.95 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | **0.41** [0.36, 0.47] | completed |

The spread comes from steep skill gradients in the game itself. The designer's ablations found that bots which walk into visible enemies lose about 100% of games to bots that do not, and that exact knowledge of the hidden tide is worth about an 80% win rate against the next rung down (`phase4/pilot/F4/revealed/sealed/DESIGN_NOTES.md`). Opus's 509-line bot carries an empirically measured table of tide profiles for every period from 20 to 40 and filters hypotheses about the cycle, which is the intended construct of inferring hidden dynamics by play [my reading of `answers/opus/bot.py`; a passphrase copy on disk means sealed-file access cannot be ruled out]. Snapshots were taken at different stages, so the Sonnet-Fable-Opus ordering is not a fair comparison; only the Haiku-to-Opus span is robust.

**Why it ranks first.** Tournaments are an old, well-understood measurement technology: thousands of games give tight per-bot CIs, the opponent ladder can be raised indefinitely, and the exploits R6 found (play-time compute, co-seating, anchor fingerprinting, decompiling the engine) all have standard tournament fixes. The pilot already showed the per-move CPU cap is easy to enforce. Writing a bot is also the kind of general agentic engineering the A2 archetype is meant to reward, so loading on general capability is a feature here [R7]. External evidence supports both the A2 direction and the headroom. At the AtCoder World Tour Finals 2026 Heuristic, OpenAI's agent beat all 12 human finalists, scoring more than 7× the best human ([the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/openais-ai-beats-every-human-at-atcoder-a-top-competitive-programming-contest/) [S]). Yet in CodeClash, Sonnet 4.5 won **0 of 37,500 rounds** against an expert human-written bot as of Nov 2025 ([CodeClash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00839); [insight](https://codeclash.ai/insights/20251105_human_ai/) [S]), so human-programmer bots make credible top anchors. NetHackers already re-scores bots on secret seeds because "a bot can still influence its self-reported score" ([nethackers](https://github.com/dunnolab/nethackers)). The differentiators from CodeClash and ALE-Bench ([ALE-Bench](https://github.com/SakanaAI/ALE-Bench)) are a secret new adversarial game each season, an anchored ladder and private maps. The adoption risk is real: Google's Gemini 3 Pro evaluation reports Vending-Bench 2 but not Google's own Game Arena ([PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/gemini/gemini_3_pro_model_evaluation.pdf)).

### 2. Stump Arena: price how cheaply people can stump a model

| Field | Design |
|---|---|
| Archetype | **A1**, hybrid: an authoring game feeding a renewing benchmark |
| Task format | Authors build micro-items (image, video ≤ 15 s, audio, short text rendered as an image, interactive widget) whose answer follows from the item itself, not trivia, across 8 fixed clusters with quotas: counting and occlusion, motion, spatial relations, audio, fine visual comparison, physical and causal events, social intent, interactive widgets. Each scored model answers every item in its tranche once, tools off |
| Novel instances | Humans author new items continuously: open bounty authors, a controlled stratum of about 100 paid naive authors in proctored sessions, and an unranked model-author arm. An item enters the pool only if ≥ 4 of 5 naive verifiers solve it within 3 minutes and ≥ 80% of ≥ 10 fresh adults solve it on re-gate. A "hard pool" also fails a locally run, pinned open-weight panel at pass@8; closed models never see items before scoring. Quarterly seasons, monthly tranches, per-lab exposure log |
| Scoring | Exact match. Headline 1: human solve rate minus model pass@1, macro-averaged over clusters. Headline 2: **stump yield**, items humans pass and model X fails per author-hour, inverted into "cost to stump model X" and trended by season. ≥ 1,500 gated items per season |
| Expected human vs AI | Humans ≥ 85% by construction; frontier pass@1 perhaps 40–75% on the full pool and 15–40% on the hard pool at launch [speculation, anchored] |
| Expected model separation | By cluster and by lab, following the large lab reorderings seen on perception benchmarks |
| Must-fix and risks | Human headline measured on the same re-gate used to select items, biased up about 3–6 pp, so use a third, independent (ideally in-lab) human sample [R7]; score all models on the same tranche in the same window [R7]; crowd verifiers who use AI fail exactly the AI-hard items, so proctor the verifier stage and audit for model-matching wrong answers [R6, R7]; filtered pools decay, so headline only the current season and publish a decay curve against a matched control pool; freeze frame rate and resolution and set a ~1 s minimum event duration so stumps are not API frame-sampling artefacts [R7]; tool-canary items against hidden server-side vision tools [R6]; keep lab-authored items out of that lab's headline [R6]. Programme $25–40k per season; scoring $50–300 per model [estimates] |

**Why it ranks second.** Stump Arena is the only A1 design whose gap is renewed by construction rather than by hoping one task family stays hard, and its second headline is a thermometer that cannot saturate: even if the gap closes, the cost to stump keeps rising and stays informative [R7]. The construct rests on solid precedent. Adversarially authored Quizbowl questions cut strong models' relative accuracy by up to 40% while staying as easy for humans ([Wallace et al., TACL 2019](https://aclanthology.org/Q19-1029) [S]), and current perception gaps are large: BabyVision has adults at 94.1% against a best model of 49.7%, with Gemini 3 Pro 49.7, GPT-5.2 34.4 and Claude 4.5 Opus 14.2 (Jan 2026 models; [README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/UniPat-AI/BabyVision/main/README.md)), and MMSI-Video-Bench has humans at 96.4% against 38.0% ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/InternRobotics/MMSI-Video-Bench/main/README.md)). The risks are equally well documented. An estimated **33–46% of MTurk workers used LLMs** on a summarisation task ([Veselovsky et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07899)), and deterrents only cut use from 27.6% to 15.9% ([arXiv 2310.15683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.15683)). Adversarially filtered pools decay: HLE went from 3.07% for GPT-4o at launch ([HLE](https://github.com/centerforaisafety/hle)) to 67.7% for Opus 5.5 with tools about 20 months later ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-5-5); not like-for-like). Adversarial filtering also disadvantages the adversary model's own family ([Phang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.08181) [R7, S]), and the closest precedent for a human-authored adversarial programme, Dynabench, faded without sustained upkeep ([Dynabench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14337); fade per red team R4).

### 3. Unrun Lab: forecast interventions against exact simulated truth

| Field | Design |
|---|---|
| Archetype | **A2**, benchmark |
| Task format | Track S (ranking headline): black-box stochastic simulators composed from secret mechanism primitives (thresholds, delays, heavy tails, regime switches, feedback) that match no textbook model. Per simulator the model gets 10k rows of history, 20 interventions from a menu (each returns one sample path) and a CPU-capped Python sandbox, then answers questions as quantiles or probabilities; ≥ 50% are interventional and outside the data's support. Track M (seasonal): predict the effect of ~150 specified small-model training experiments that nobody has run yet |
| Novel instances | Fresh simulator instances every window from a private mechanism grammar, ≥ 30% new primitives per season and ≥ 2 held out; truth is computed after predictions lock |
| Scoring | CRPS or log score against the distribution of **10,000 rollouts**, expressed as skill from 0 (the better of an AutoML pipeline and an owner-written generic learner) to 100 (the true distribution); scores summed before dividing; ~1,000 paired forecasts per model |
| Expected human vs AI | Models with a sandbox likely beat data scientists on Track S [speculation]; Track M is untested |
| Expected model separation | Wide in principle, because truth is a distribution and outcome noise vanishes; in practice capped by the number of independent simulators |
| Must-fix and risks | Use ≥ 60 simulators with ~15 questions each: 20 simulators × 50 correlated questions give only about 50–90 effective observations [R7]; anchors must get a fixed, owner-chosen intervention design, not the model's own [R6]; generic "fit a simulator, then simulate" pipelines may bunch scores [R7]; Track M is broken for closed models, which can simply run the experiment server-side, so rank it only for locally run open weights or report it as a research signal [R6]. Track S costs $100–500 per model [estimate] |

**Why it ranks third.** Unrun Lab has the cleanest ground truth in the set, which matters because novel expert-written items are error-prone: 42% of FrontierMath problems needed fixes ([Epoch](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/frontiermath-tier-4-v2)). Its pilot is inconclusive: on 5 simulators and 15 questions, only Haiku finished, at skill **9.6** (90% CI −23.1 to 34.4) on a 0 = no-change, 100 = oracle scale, below a naive extrapolator's **16.0** (`phase4/pilot/F7/public/VALIDATION.md`, `PILOT_SUMMARY.md`). That shows only that a weak tier does not clear naive baselines. Two warnings apply. Public simulator families become training environments fast: NewtonBench's altered-law family was listed in NVIDIA's NeMo Gym about 4.5 months after release ([NeMo Gym](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/Gym/main/README.md)), so the grammar must stay private and rotate. And forecasting is already near human parity: Forecasting Research Institute reports AI has likely reached superforecaster parity on ForecastBench ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/ai-models-have-likely-reached-parity) [S]), which supports the A2 direction but makes the story less novel.

### 4. Compaction Chronicle: memory half-life over two-million-token streams (conditional)

| Field | Design |
|---|---|
| Archetype | **A2**, benchmark |
| Task format | A secret world simulator emits about 2M tokens in 400 chunks (knob: 200k–4M). After each chunk the model sees only that chunk, its own notes file (16 KB cap; sweeps at 4 and 64 KB) and a fixed prompt, and rewrites the notes. At 60 random checkpoints a separate call answers ~5 queries from the notes alone. Streams include retroactive corrections, unit changes, aliases and sources whose reliability is revealed later |
| Novel instances | ≥ 3 private simulator families per season with rotating domains and update semantics; ≥ 2 held-out query families; query-relevant state kept at ≥ 4× the notes cap |
| Scoring | As specified, "memory half-life" (fact age at which accuracy halves). Replace it with area under chance-corrected accuracy versus fact age, normalised to an oracle-notes ceiling, because the half-life is relative to the model's own fresh accuracy and a trivial "keep 20% of entities" policy makes it unbounded [R6] |
| Expected human vs AI | A2 by construction at full length, since humans cannot read 2M tokens; frontier models likely beat the median human on a 30k-token version [speculation], though that arm says little about the headline task [R7] |
| Expected model separation | Wide [speculation, anchored on compaction results below] |
| Conditions for inclusion | Far more than the specified 5 seeds, because a 5-cluster bootstrap under-covers [R7]; a notes-sufficiency audit and notes-swap canaries to catch hidden server-side memory on closed endpoints [R6]; ≥ 50% of queries drawn uniformly over state facts; a clear differentiation from BEAM, LongMemEval-V2, StreamMemBench and the Compaction Cliff paper [R7]. Cost $300–1,000 per model per track [estimate], about $2k with cap sweeps [R7] |

**Why it is conditional.** It had the most round-1 support of any finalist (4 keeps, 1 revise) and targets the variable that decided ARC-AGI-3: the same model at the same effort scored **62.7% on ARC's standard harness and 98.6% on a state-preserving harness** ([ARC Astra post, translation copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lihenair/techtranslate/821908553b702d9de6565d186fdd2e8661303f6f/archive/2026-09-05/ai/OpenAIs-GPT-6-Astra-on-ARC-AGI-3.md)), and OpenAI reported that retained reasoning plus compaction "tripled" its ARC-AGI-3 scores ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/how-two-settings-tripled-our-arc-agi-3-scores/) [S]). The Compaction Cliff paper reports safety-rule recall of 53% after one compaction round and 10% after five ([arXiv 2608.22752](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22752) [S; exact quote uncertain]). But both round-2 reviewers found its headline broken as written, R7 would drop it for crowding and low power, and it is unpiloted. Treat it as the first alternate rather than a co-equal pick.

## Pilots killed three finalists, and human advantages are running out

The three cuts are useful negative results because each failed on the assumption that made it attractive. **Hidden-Rule Lab** was the provisional favourite on the strength of 2025–26 studies in which humans out-explored AI. Its static, image-only pilot asked solvers to infer a hidden visual rule from labelled scenes: **Sonnet and Opus scored 64/64 and wrote out all 8 hidden rules exactly**, while Haiku scored 31/64 (48.4%), at chance, below the best shallow script's 59.4% (`phase4/pilot/PILOT_SUMMARY.md`; `F2/public/VALIDATION.md`). Recognising a rule in clean rendered scenes is no longer a human moat; only the untested interactive version, with a bounded adversary and at least 60 human baselines, could revive the A1 claim, and R6 found its median-based headline rewards gambling on early answers. **Patch Auditor** asked models to find property-violating code patches and prove each with a witness input. Fable, Opus and Sonnet all scored a perfect 1.000 and Haiku 0.444, while a differential fuzzer with two normalisations permitted by the public specs also scored 1.000 (an upper bound, since it used the owner's input generators; `F3/RESULTS.md`). Because benign patches were behaviour-preserving refactors, "did behaviour change at all?" separated the classes, and violations diverged on 11–65% of random inputs. The witness requirement worked (no label disputes), so a redesign would keep it but add benign behaviour changes and sparse violations. **Self-Knowledge Exam** asked models to forecast their own success. Sonnet, Opus and Fable scored 60/60, so the calibration metrics only rewarded confidence near 1 and ranked Fable last for being cautious; Haiku's discrimination (AUROC 0.71) fell to 0.55 within a difficulty level, below what the visible difficulty cue alone achieves (0.74); and **deliberately failing 3 items lifted AUROC and triage to 1.00** (`F5/RESULTS.md`). R7 cites a 2026 study of 20 models finding that LLM confidence "largely reduces to a shared difficulty heuristic" ([arXiv 2605.24299](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24299) [R7, S]).

The F2 result points to a broader problem. **Durable human-over-AI gaps are now scarce and concentrated in perception, intuitive physics, exploration, real-time play and cross-episode learning**, and most measurements predate the Sep 2026 frontier. IntPhys 2 has humans at 96.44% against a best model of 57.51% (Jun 2025; [arXiv 2506.09849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09849) [S]); 517 humans beat o3, Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude 4 Sonnet at reward-free world-model learning on AutumnBench ([arXiv 2510.19788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.19788) [S]); ZendoWorld has humans at 73.3% against 44.5% for vision-language agents ([arXiv 2607.08233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08233) [S]); VideoGameBench agents completed 0.48% in real time ([arXiv 2505.18134](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18134) [S]); and on CL-bench, learning from new context, the best model reached 23.7% with no human baseline ([arXiv 2602.03587](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03587)). Protocol-bound gaps are fragile: BALROG's fixed NetHack protocol still holds GPT-6 Astra to about 13% progression ([PR #19](https://github.com/balrog-ai/experiments/pull/19)), yet Astra ascended in the full game with web access and tools it built itself ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kenforthewin/nethack_astra/main/README.md)). Targeted gaps close fast: ARC-AGI-3 went from under 1% to 99.9% in about 5 months, with Astra using fewer actions than the median human on 96% of levels ([translation copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lihenair/techtranslate/821908553b702d9de6565d186fdd2e8661303f6f/archive/2026-09-05/ai/OpenAIs-GPT-6-Astra-on-ARC-AGI-3.md)), and the best ClockBench score rose from 13.3% to 66.7% in about 12 months ([launch](https://the-decoder.com/even-the-best-ai-models-cant-reliably-read-the-clock/); [2026](https://theslowai.substack.com/p/ai-clock-benchmark-capacity) [S]). The only A1 finalist left, Stump Arena, is unpiloted; the next A1 idea in reserve, First-Run Arcade (learning unknown real-time games from pixels), was held back because paused play follows ARC-AGI-3's collapse path and similar arenas already exist. An honest summary is that A2 designs are now much easier to make robust than A1 designs.

## Benchmarks die from cheap shortcuts and absent owners, not only memorisation

**Verdict on your hypothesis: partly right, wrong as a single cause.** Panel C coded 36 benchmark cases: under the "unseen instances" reading, **5 support, 10 contradict and 21 are neutral**; under a "novel skills" reading it shifts to about 9 / 6 / 21 (`phase1/C_failed_saturated.md` §4). The counts are coding-sensitive, so the verdict rests on three counterexamples that hold under every reading. **ARC-AGI-3**, a novel interactive family with no item leakage, went from under 1% to 99.9% in about 5 months, with the last jump coming from a state-preserving harness rather than from training on items. **FrontierMath Tier 4**, built from unpublished problems, went from 5% to 98% in under 14 months, with the caveats that the 98% is on an error-fixed v2 and that mathematicians reported AI "unintended shortcuts" ([Epoch on X](https://x.com/EpochAIResearch/status/2098103831502708864)). **ARC-AGI-1** had 49% of its private set solved by a 2020 ensemble of brute-force program searches ([ARC Prize 2024 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04604)). The converse also fails: GPQA, SWE-bench and AutomationBench stayed useful without novel skills. The kernel of your hypothesis is real for static public items. AIME 2024 scores run 10–20 points above what AIME 2025 predicts ([MathArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23281); size uncertain), and OpenAI retired SWE-bench Verified after frontier models reproduced gold patches or verbatim problem details for some tasks ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/)). Novel skills also buy time: ARC-AGI-1 took about four years to go from 0% to 5%.

The refined statement is that **benchmarks fail when the cheapest way to raise the score stops running through the named capability, and no owner renews items, families and protocol faster than labs optimise against them**. Novelty closes one cheap path, not the others:

| Failure mode | Does novelty prevent it? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Item contamination | Yes | AIME 2024; SWE-bench Verified |
| Training on the task family | Only while the generator stays secret | Bulls-and-Cows ladder saturated in ~9.5 weeks ([commits](https://github.com/stalkermustang/llm-bulls-and-cows-benchmark/commits/main)); Logic-RL hit 0.99 after ~5k synthetic puzzles ([arXiv 2502.14768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14768)) |
| Brute force or writing a solver | No | ARC-AGI-1's 49% ensemble |
| Harness capture | No | ARC-AGI-3 62.7% vs 98.6%; OSWorld Sonnet 4.5 42.88% at 15 steps vs 62.88% at 100 ([xlsx](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/os-world/os-world.github.io/main/static/data/osworld_verified_results.xlsx)) |
| Broken validity | No; novel expert sets are more error-prone | Do-nothing agent scores 38% on τ-bench ([repo](https://github.com/uiuc-kang-lab/agentic-benchmarks)); >65% of HellaSwag predictions unchanged on "Lorem ipsum" ([arXiv 2504.07825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07825)) |
| Gaming and selective submission | No | Meta privately tested 27 Llama 4 variants on LMArena ([Leaderboard Illusion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879)) |
| Noise and thin baselines | No | Median human baseline across 115 reviewed is 8 people ([Wei et al.](https://github.com/kevinlwei/human-baselines)); a 3-pp gap needs ~1,000 items ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)) |
| Adoption failure | No | OfficeBench; most of your "failed" list |

What succeeds is distribution plus trust. **"Automation Bench" is Zapier's AutomationBench** ([arXiv 2604.18934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18934); [repo](https://github.com/zapier/AutomationBench)): a "boring", non-novel skill with deterministic end-state grading, a harder private split that Zapier re-hardens between versions, and neutral runners. ## Method and limits: about 870 fact-checked claims and one lab's pilots

The research ran in four phases. **Phase 1** had seven panels (A human-beats-AI, B AI-beats-human but discriminative, C failed and saturated benchmarks plus the hypothesis test, D games, E capability gaps, F test-time learning, G methodology), each followed by an independent fact-check: about 465 claim rows, of which 322 were verified, 74 corrected, 67 left uncertain and 1 removed. **Phase 2** synthesised 20 design principles, a 12-mode failure taxonomy, a red-team rubric and the hypothesis verdict, fact-checked over 71 rows (42 verified, 23 corrected, 6 uncertain). **Phase 3** used four ideation panels (human-beats-AI, discriminative AI-beats-human, games, wildcard) to produce 47 candidates, fact-checked over 115 rows (81 verified, 14 corrected, 20 uncertain). **Phase 4** used five round-1 red teams (trainability, separation, baselines and scoring, prior art and interest, holistic), fact-checked over 223 rows (182 verified, 21 corrected, 12 uncertain, 1 removed, 7 not re-checked); a revision panel that selected seven finalists; two round-2 reviewers (R6, R7) whose files were **not** independently fact-checked; and blinded pilots of five finalists. Fact-checkers removed only two claims across all phases.

Access was the main evidential constraint. The egress proxy blocked arxiv.org, arcprize.org, huggingface.co, metr.org, epoch.ai, openreview.net, openai.com, kaggle.com, simple-bench.com, andonlabs.com, aclanthology.org and atcoder.jp, among others, and panels exhausted their shared search quotas after roughly 11–40 queries each. Primary evidence therefore came from raw GitHub files, verbatim captures and mirrors, and anthropic.com; claims seen only through search summaries are labelled [S] and should be read as medium confidence. The pilots have their own limits: all solvers were Claude tiers (Haiku, Sonnet, Opus, Fable) from one lab, run in the Claude Code agent harness rather than a frozen benchmark harness, with model versions and budgets not pinned; samples were tiny (8 rule problems, 20 patches, 60 items, 5 simulators, 100 games per rung); there were no human participants; tool policies ran on the honour system; solvers could in principle have read other solvers' files and, for F4, a local tool-results file that contained the decryption passphrase; and usage limits interrupted several runs, including every F4 solver except Haiku and every F7 solver except Haiku.

## Conclusion

The search for a benchmark that "AI can't have trained on" is the wrong target, because every fixed target gets trained on within months once it matters. The designs that survived are the ones where renewal is the mechanism rather than an afterthought: a new game every season, new human authors every month, new simulators every window, each scored against anchors that can be raised. That favours A2 over A1. Season Forge shows that a single novel, steep-gradient game separates model tiers by several ladder rungs at modest scoring cost, while the pilots showed that clean, static reasoning tasks, the kind ARC-AGI made famous, now saturate at mid-tier. The human-beats-AI archetype survives mainly as a measurement of how fast gaps close, which is exactly what Stump Arena's cost-to-stump headline records.

The most useful next steps follow directly. Re-run Season Forge across at least three labs with a frozen harness, 8–10 attempts per model, search-bot and human-programmer top anchors and a per-move compute cap, and check whether frontier neighbours separate. Run a Stump Arena pilot of a few hundred items with a proctored, in-lab human headline sample before committing to its A1 claim, since that pilot is what would turn the shortlist's only human-beats-AI idea from a forecast into evidence.
