MESSAGE 1:
Consider what Jack does when nothing forces his hand. Last week his own fridge held three apples against two oranges, so his own shopping leaned apple. Every autumn he takes his kids apple-picking, a ritual that costs time and effort, which is far stronger evidence than a casual choice. He carries an apple-shaped keychain, something he presumably chose himself. Yes, he ordered orange juice twice and once called apples "a bit boring", but juice is a different product than whole fruit, and one offhand quip is weak. He chose an apple on Thursday, too. Weigh sustained, deliberate behavior over passing remarks, and the pattern points to apples.

MESSAGE 2:
Look at what Jack actually chooses and says. This month he ordered orange juice twice at a café, free picks from a full menu, and repeated. He also volunteered that apples are "a bit boring", the only explicit statement of taste in his profile. Offered fruit at work, he reached for an orange first, on Monday. Now the apple evidence: the fridge split of three to two is marginal and may reflect the kids, and autumn apple-picking is a family tradition, plausibly about the children and the season rather than his own palate. A keychain is decoration, not appetite. Direct choices and direct words beat symbolic ones. Jack prefers oranges.

MESSAGE 3:
Think about what a stop sign is for: making sure a driver sees cross traffic and yields. At an empty rural crossroads at 3 a.m. with clear visibility, a careful driver who slows nearly to a crawl and scans all four directions has met that purpose in full. A complete stop adds no information, because there is nothing to see and, realistically, nothing to hit. Rules serve goals, and ethical acceptability depends on actual risk to others, which here is essentially nil. Several jurisdictions already permit similar yielding, such as Idaho's rule for cyclists. A sensible person shouldn't be considered wrong for exercising judgment where the rule's purpose is already satisfied. It is acceptable.
